25  Entites in the Ideal Sphere

This book has had a strong focus on the real sphere of existence, which is the domain of physical objects and events. However, it is equally important to consider the ideal sphere, which encompasses entities that are not bound by space and time. These entities are abstract, universal, and timeless, and they play a crucial role in our understanding of reality. The focus on the real sphere is however justified because the entities in the ideal sphere depend on entities and processes in the real sphere for their initial existence. For example, mathematical objects are abstract entities that exist independently of the physical world, but they are often discovered through the study of real-world phenomena and by entities (such as people) who encounter them. Similarly, ethical and aesthetic values are abstract concepts that guide human behavior and decision-making, but they are often informed by real-world experiences and observations.

In the ontology of the German philosopher Nicolai Hartmann, the universe is split into two primary, disjunctive modes of being: the real sphere (things existing in space and time) and the ideal sphere (timeless, non-spatial essences). [1, 2] Within Hartmann’s Ideal Sphere (ideales Sein), entities do not vanish, change, or possess physical structure. Instead, they are defined as universal, self-contained, and free from contradiction. Two primary types of entities exist within this sphere: [2, 3]

  • Mathematical Objects: This includes geometric structures (like a perfect circle or a triangle), numbers, sets, and purely logical relations. They possess their own objective, independent reality that human minds merely discover rather than invent. [2, 4]
  • Values (Ethical and Aesthetic contents): Hartmann developed an objective axiological realism, meaning he believed that moral values (such as justice, courage, and honesty) and aesthetic values (such as beauty and harmony) have an absolute, transhistorical existence. They act as absolute standards that exist independently of whether humans recognize or practice them. [2, 5]

25.1 Key Characteristics of Entities in the Ideal Sphere:

  • Timelessness: Unlike real entities (which are individual, destructible, and chained to temporal events), ideal entities are permanent and indestructible. They do emerge from the real sphere at a time in the sphere, but once established in the ideal sphere there is no dynamic process. Changes in th eideal sphere are due to acts of creation or distruction on the real sphere [2]
  • Generality: Real things are always unique individuals, while ideal things are always universal. [2, 3]
  • Freedom from Contradiction: While a real object can face material resistance and physical conflict, the strict requirement for an entity to exist in the ideal sphere is its absolute internal logical coherence. [2]

In Nicolai Hartmann’s philosophy, the ideal sphere and the real sphere are fundamentally separated by a metaphysical chasm. However, they intersect directly through human consciousness and spirit (Geist). Hartmann argues that ideal entities cannot physically cause things to happen on their own. Instead, they require a human mediator to “bring” them into the real world. This interaction happens through three primary mechanisms:

25.2 1. The Powerlessness of the Ideal (Die Ohnmacht des Idealen)

A crucial rule in Hartmann’s ontology is that the higher sphere is always weaker than the lower sphere.

  • The Real Sphere is strong. It contains material forces, causal laws, and physical energy.
  • The Ideal Sphere is completely powerless (ohnmächtig). A mathematical law or an ethical value (like justice) cannot physically force itself into existence. It has no causal energy.

Because values and mathematical truths cannot act on the world, they are completely dependent on the lower, real strata of reality to manifest.

25.3 2. Teleology and the Human Bridge

The intersection between the two spheres happens exclusively within human spirit and consciousness, which belong to the highest layer of the real sphere. Humans act as a transformational bridge through a process Hartmann calls teleological activity (purposeful action):

  • Primary Discovery (Vision): Human beings possess a unique capacity to look into the ideal sphere. We grasp mathematical laws through intellectual intuition, and we feel moral values through an innate “emotional value-sense” (Wertgefühl).
  • Setting the Goal: Once a human perceives an ideal value (e.g., the ideal of justice), they can adopt it as a conscious goal (Zweck).
  • Realisation: The human uses their own physical body, energy, and the causal laws of the material world to reshape reality, turning that ideal goal into a real, historical fact (e.g., writing a just law or building a fair institution).

25.4 3. The “Over-Forming” of Reality (Überformung)

When the ideal sphere interacts with the real world through human actions, it does not destroy the laws of nature. Instead, it over-forms them. For example, when an engineer uses ideal mathematical and geometric laws to build a bridge, the physical stones still obey the real law of gravity. However, those stones have been arranged according to an ideal geometric plan. The ideal sphere has superimposed a new, higher form of order onto the material world. Similarly, when an individual acts courageously, they use their physical body and psychological willpower (real sphere) to give material shape to a timeless moral value (ideal sphere).
